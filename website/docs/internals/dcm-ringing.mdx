# DCM Coil Ringing

In discontinuous conduction mode (DCM), the LS rectifier turns off when the inductor current
reaches zero. The residual energy in the inductor then resonates with the parasitic capacitance
at the switch node (MOSFET C_oss, LS FET junction capacitance, and PCB trace capacitance), which
forms an LC tank. In a synchronous buck with diode emulation (this firmware, `src/buck.h`), the
LS FET's C_oss is the dominant capacitance and the main inductor L is the tank inductance.

This page covers what happens *after* the LS FET turns off at the zero crossing. The companion
page [Diode Emulation](diode-emulation.md) covers the ZCD timing.

## Two kinds of switch-node ringing

The switch node shows two ringing phenomena, and the DCM coil ring is distinct from the HF
transition ring:

- **HF transition ringing** occurs at *every* HS/LS switching edge. The *parasitic loop
  inductance* (trace + package ~10–30 nH) resonates with C_oss at tens to hundreds of MHz. The
  ring lasts a few ns, and snubbers, gate-R, and layout address it. Most app notes (TI slyt465,
  Specter Engineering, ADI) focus on this ring.
- **DCM coil ringing** (this page) occurs *only after* the inductor current reaches zero and
  both FETs are off. The main power inductor L resonates with the switch-node capacitance C_sw
  at f_r ≈ 1/(2π√(L·C_sw)), which is well above f_sw (39 kHz) but far below the HF transition
  ring. The ring lasts hundreds of ns to µs. Its lower frequency makes firmware active damping
  feasible, because T_ring/4 is comfortably within MCPWM tick resolution.

## Ring frequency per board

C_sw is per-board and depends mostly on how many FETs are paralleled. It is not a universal
constant. A C_sw of ~100–200 pF, which gives 1.3–2 MHz, is roughly an order of magnitude low for any
board with paralleled 100 V FETs.

The following table compares three example boards (A, B, C):

| board | L | C_sw | f_r | T_ring/4 |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| A (Fugu2-class, 50–80 µH coils) | 50–80 µH | ~100–200 pF *(estimate, unverified)* | 1.3–2 MHz | 125–192 ns |
| **B** | **83 µH** (measured, DMM6500 4-wire) | **2.98–3.04 nF** (measured 2026-09-06) | **320 kHz** | **~780 ns** |
| C | 40 µH (`coil.conf L0`) | **unmeasured** (see [Board C](#board-c-node-capacitance)) | — | — |

Board B carries a single IPP022N12NM6 on the low side. Board C carries 2× IPP050N10NF2S (HS) and
2× IPP039N10N5 (LS), which puts four 100 V dies on the node. C_oss rises steeply toward 0 V,
which is precisely where this ring lives.

### How to measure the ring

A valid measurement of C_sw follows three rules.

1. Run the converter in diode emulation, not forced PWM. Forced PWM holds the LS on through the
   zero crossing and abolishes the ring entirely.
2. Trigger on the LS gate's falling edge. The LS gate is ground-referenced and driven every cycle
   regardless of what the power stage does, so it is the only sound arming point.

   The switch node can't trigger its own measurement. `scope_backends.mxo.capture()` defaults to
   a *slew-rate* trigger (`max_transition_s = 100e-9`) that by construction fires only on
   transitions faster than 100 ns, and this ring's transit is of order 1 µs. A run armed on the
   node therefore records the HS turn-on edge and looks like a successful ring capture. An
   earlier capture on board B did exactly that. Its rise durations fall 217 → 132 ns across
   Vin 35–70 V, and it was mistaken for this measurement for three weeks.
3. Fit the resonance instead of inverting a quarter-cycle. Fit
   `v(t) = V_c − A·cos(ω(t − t₀))` over the unclamped part of the rise (3–93 %) and take
   `C_sw = 1/(ω²L)`.

   Inverting `T/4 = (π/2)·√(L·C_sw)` over the 0 → Vin transit gives a wrong `C_sw` for this
   waveform. The transit is not a quarter-cycle starting from rest. The node swings about a
   *midpoint* and is truncated when the HS body diode clamps it one V_f above V_in, so `dV/dt`
   peaks near mid-swing rather than at the rail.

### Board B measurement (2026-09-06)

The board B value comes from a capture with the output open, near-zero current, Vin 71.10 V, and
`dc 200` (D = 0.049). An MXO44 had C1 on the switch node and C2 on the LS gate, armed on the
gate's falling edge, and recorded 12 events.

The fit gives `ω = 2.012e6 ± 9.6e3 rad/s` (sd 0.5 % across events), `V_c = 37.84 ± 0.11 V`,
`A = 41.96 ± 0.18 V`, and `f_r = 320.2 kHz`. The swing would reach `V_c + A` = 79.8 V and is
clamped at **72.84 ± 0.10 V**, one diode drop above V_in. With L = 83 µH (measured), C_sw is
**2.977 nF**. With `A_L·N²` = 81.2 µH, it is **3.042 nF**.

### Board C node capacitance

Board C's C_sw is unmeasured. Its existing captures are two Vin sweeps (run2 and run3) armed on the
switch node, and they don't fit a resonant quarter-cycle.

The following table shows that the 0 → Vin transit in those traces depends on Vin:

| Vin (V) | 14.9 | 20.2 | 25.3 | 30.4 | 35.7 | 40.9 | 46.0 |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| run2 (ns) | 781 | 695 | 633 | 589 | 623 | 598 | 546 |
| run3 (ns) | 833 | 712 | 677 | 524 | 547 | 533 | 479 |

The transit falls monotonically by 30 % (run2) and 43 % (run3). Board C's `dV/dt` also peaks at
50–60 % of swing, like board B's, where a quarter-cycle from rest must peak at the rail.

Refitting these traces with the resonant model gives a `C_sw` that drifts 7.97 → 3.88 nF with Vin.
The fit diverges outright on run3's three lowest points (`V_c` = −5582 V, f = 8.4 kHz, a cosine
fitted to a nearly straight segment). At Vin 46 the candidates span 2.3–4.4 nF. A fresh
acquisition by the gate-triggered method above would settle the value.

### Open question: resonance or constant-current charge

Board B's value also has an open question. A straight line fits the central 15–85 % of *every*
rise in these captures to R² ≈ 0.999, board B's included. Over that span a cosine and a ramp are
barely distinguishable. Board B's number rests on its fit converging where board C's does not,
plus the body-diode clamp. It does **not** rest on having excluded a constant-current charge.

A Vin sweep with the gate trigger would separate the two models. A resonance gives a transit
that is invariant with Vin, and a current-driven charge gives one proportional to Vin. That sweep
has not been run.

The constant-current model (`I_neg = Vout·t_LS/L` pumped by the low-side minimum on-time) is
therefore an open alternative, because the board C transit in the table above depends on Vin.

## Reduction techniques

### 1. RC snubber (hardware)

An RC snubber is a series R+C from the switch node to ground, placed as close to the LS FET as
possible. The standard design procedure (ADI, TI, Severns) has these steps:

1. Measure the ringing frequency f_r on the switch node with a scope (bandwidth limiting off,
   short ground spring).
2. Find the parasitic C. Add capacitance C_ext from SW to GND until f_r drops to **half**. Then
   C_parasitic = C_ext / 3 (total C quadrupled → f halved, since f ∝ 1/√(LC)).
3. Find the parasitic L: L_parasitic = 1 / (4π² · f_r² · C_parasitic).
4. Set R_snubber = √(L_parasitic / C_parasitic), the characteristic impedance of the tank. This
   value critically damps it.
5. Set C_snubber = 3–10× C_parasitic (ADI says 1–4×, TI says 3–10×). A larger C gives more
   damping but more loss.
6. Estimate the snubber loss, P = ½ · C_snub · V² · f_sw, the efficiency penalty per cycle.

An EE StackExchange thread on an LM2576 buck describes practical tuning. The calculations give
an approximate value, and the final value should be found empirically: solder in a pot and tune
R for the best waveform. The RC time constant should be ≥ 3× the ringing rise time. Don't aim for
critical damping, because a "tidy waveform" costs more efficiency than it's worth. The final
values for that example were 1 nF + 470 Ω.

For a nonsynchronous buck, the ringing voltage never exceeds the switching voltage, so it's
primarily an EMI concern, not a reliability one. The same applies in a synchronous buck: the ring
is bounded by the tank energy, not by V_in.

### 2. Gate resistance tuning (hardware / firmware)

A higher LS FET turn-off gate resistance slows dv/dt at the moment the FET opens. This reduces
the excitation energy injected into the LC tank, at the cost of higher switching loss. Specter
Engineering showed a near-linear decrease in overshoot vs. gate resistance from 1–20 Ω.

In this firmware the MCPWM dead-time pair and gate driver strength already influence this.
Adjusting the dead-time can marginally affect ringing amplitude.

### 3. Layout optimization (hardware)

A smaller high-di/dt commutation loop lowers overshoot, so keep the input capacitor, HS FET, and
LS FET as physically close as possible. Trace adds ~10 nH per 25 mm. A smaller loop means less
parasitic L, less stored energy, and lower overshoot. This is the most effective hardware change
but requires a board respin. Planar/laminated busbars or adjacent PCB power layers add magnetic
field cancellation.

### 4. Forced CCM (firmware)

In continuous conduction mode the inductor current never reaches zero, so DCM ringing never
occurs. The converter can stay in CCM in several ways:

- **Minimum preload** (bleeder resistor on the output): the converter always draws enough
  current to stay in CCM. This is simple but wastes power continuously.
- **Force CCM in firmware**: keep the LS FET on even at zero current, allowing negative
  inductor current (forced PWM). This eliminates DCM ringing entirely but introduces
  circulating current losses, which at light loads can be worse than the ringing.

  This firmware provides it through `converter.conf::forced_pwm=1`, or `sync forced` on the
  console (manual PWM only, `dc N` first). With the default `fpwm_gate=1` this only *requests*
  forced PWM, which takes effect once the duty reaches the voltage ratio (see
  [`fpwm_gate`](../reference/config/converter.md#fpwm_gate-fpwm_gate_margin)). Once effective
  (`forcedPwm_()` in `src/buck.h`), `computeDCM` returns false and the LS runs complementary.
  At a light-load DCM point the duty is below the voltage ratio, so the gated
  `sync forced`/`forced_pwm=1` does not engage there by itself.

  `sync forced!` (or `fpwm_gate=0`) engages immediately. That is **only safe on an output that
  cannot sink**, because forced PWM lets the inductor current go negative.
- **Higher inductance** (hardware): lowers the critical load boundary
  (I_crit = ΔI_L / 2 ∝ 1/L), extending CCM to lower loads. The trade-offs are a larger coil,
  more copper loss, less saturation headroom, and slower current slew. Lower inductance does the
  opposite.

### 5. Pulse skipping / burst mode (firmware)

At light loads, the converter can skip switching pulses entirely instead of operating in DCM. It
fires a burst of CCM pulses to charge the output, then goes idle until V_out droops below a
threshold. Each pulse is a CCM pulse, so DCM ringing doesn't occur. The trade-offs are
lower-frequency output ripple at the burst frequency and potential audible noise. Many IC
controllers (onsemi FAN65008, TI LM5146) do this automatically.

In this firmware this could be implemented as a minimum PWM duty floor. If the computed duty
falls below a threshold, the firmware either holds PWM at 0 (skip) or fires a minimum-width CCM
pulse periodically.

### 6. Active damping via LS FET (firmware, specific to synchronous buck)

The ringing is between L and C_oss of the LS FET. Instead of letting it ring freely after the LS
FET turns off, the firmware can use the FETs to absorb the energy:

- **Keep the LS FET on briefly** after zero-current detection to clamp the switch node to
  ground, dissipating the residual energy in R_ds(on) before releasing it. The FET acts as a
  controlled dissipative element. This is the most promising firmware-only approach for this
  project. The existing `SynchronousConverter` (`src/buck.h`) already computes diode-emulation
  timing, and a brief LS-FET re-trigger pulse at the first ring valley (~T_ring/4 after LS-off)
  could be added.

  The valley time is per-board. Per the C_sw table above, it is ~125–192 ns on board A
  (estimate, unverified), but **~780 ns on board B** (measured 2026-09-06). A re-trigger
  hard-coded to board A's timing would fire at roughly the ring's peak on board B, i.e. pump
  energy in rather than clamp it out. Derive this delay from the board's own `L0` and its FET
  population, not from a constant. Board C has no usable valley time until its C_sw is
  measured.

  The PWM drivers and existing settings relate to this approach as follows:

  - **MCPWM**: the ESP32-S3 has 2 comparators per operator (`SOC_MCPWM_COMPARATORS_PER_OPERATOR=2`),
    both already used for HS and LS edges (`cmpHS_`, `cmpLS_` in `src/pwm/mcpwm.h`). An LS
    re-trigger pulse would need a different mechanism, e.g. a cross-triggered comparator on a
    second operator, the dead-time submodule, or a software-triggered one-shot event. This is
    feasible but not trivial, since no spare hardware comparator is available.
  - **LEDC**: not feasible. LEDC uses 2 channels (one for HS, one for LS), each producing
    exactly one pulse per period via duty/hpoint registers. There is no event system for
    generating an additional edge.
  - **`boot_refresh_ns` / `pwmRectMin`** (2000 ns default, `src/buck.h:574`) is a *minimum LS
    on-time* for HS bootstrap cap refresh. It keeps LS on *before* the ring starts, not after,
    so it is not a ring damper. Active damping needs a *new* LS re-trigger pulse timed to the
    ring valley.
  - **`rect_offset_ns`** (`coil.conf`, `src/buck.h:588`) already provides partial damping. It
    delays LS turn-off slightly past the true zero crossing, so a small reverse current flows,
    loading the tank and dissipating ring energy. It is calibrated per board (two Fugu2 boards:
    +100 and +78 counts). Increasing it further damps more but pushes the operating point toward
    the reverse-current cliff (see [Diode Emulation](diode-emulation.md)).
- **Turn the HS FET on early** (before the next nominal cycle) to clamp the switch node to V_in,
  absorbing the ring energy into the input cap. This requires precise timing and is used in some
  advanced controllers.
- **Briefly re-turn the LS FET on** for a few ns at the zero-crossing to short the tank. This is
  the "active clamp" approach (see US8933635B2 patent for LED driver application).

### 7. Schottky diode in parallel with LS FET body diode (hardware)

An external Schottky diode has near-zero reverse recovery current, which reduces the current
step that excites the RLC tank at turn-off. Specter Engineering's simulation showed ~90 V
overshoot reduction. This is a BOM change, not firmware.

## Summary

The following table compares the reduction techniques:

| Technique | HW/FW | Effectiveness | Efficiency cost | No board change? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RC snubber | HW | High | Low–moderate (½CV²f) | No |
| Gate R tuning | HW/FW | Medium | Moderate | Partially (dead-time) |
| Layout optimization | HW | High | None | No (respin) |
| Forced CCM | FW | Complete | High at light load | Yes |
| Pulse skipping / burst | FW | High | Low | Yes |
| Active damping (LS keep-on) | FW | High | Low | Yes (experimental) |
| Schottky parallel diode | HW | Medium | Low | No |

For this project, the most promising approaches that need no hardware change are burst/pulse-skip
at light load and active damping via a brief LS-FET keep-alive pulse at the zero crossing. Both
build on the existing diode-emulation logic in `src/buck.h`.
